## 🛑 The Problem

In previous investigative roles, teams regularly encountered systemic gaps that contributed to significant losses. We would participate in post-mortem discussions, identify exactly what went wrong, and provide feedback on how to fix it. In many cases, though, the conversation ended there. The problem was understood, the recommendation was made—and then shelved.

I've always looked for opportunities to bring a more proactive mindset to work that is often reactive by nature.

Fraud is a constant game of cat and mouse, which means the ability to adapt quickly matters. Yet, turning an investigator's insight into action can be a slow process. By the time feedback has been heard, evaluated, prioritized, and built into the roadmap, we're already off chasing a new mouse.

---

## 💡 The Solution

I started thinking about how much faster we could pivot if investigators were able to validate their own feedback before an enhancement ever enters the development pipeline. Instead of sending an underdeveloped idea downstream, investigators could build a basic proof of concept, test it, break it, fix it, and determine whether it actually has legs.

That became my motivation for learning the technical skills to test my own ideas and build lightweight investigative tools.

The goal is not to replace engineering teams. It is to strengthen an investigator's concept before it reaches them—filtering out what doesn't work so technical teams can focus on what does. This gives investigators the autonomy to work through the early stages of an idea and determine whether it is viable enough to pursue.
